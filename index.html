Two jobs, two kinds of models

Imagine a pile of cat and dog photos. You can learn to sort them, or you can learn to draw new ones. That is the whole difference.

Discriminative: The Detective

Looks at something and decides which group it belongs to. It does not care what a cat looks like in general, only where the boundary between cats and dogs sits.

Is this photo a cat or a dog?

  • Gives you: a label or a probability, such as "91% dog".
  • Typical models: logistic regression, decision trees, support vector machines and neural-network classifiers.
  • Strength: usually very accurate at sorting, and simpler to train.
  • Limit: it cannot create new examples, because it never learns what a cat looks like, only how cats differ from dogs.
Learns P(label | data)

Generative: The Artist

Studies many examples until it learns what each group looks like, then uses that knowledge to create brand-new ones. It models how the data itself is spread out.

Show me a cat that never existed.

  • Gives you: new data, such as images, text or audio.
  • Typical models: Naive Bayes, variational autoencoders (VAEs), GANs, diffusion models and large language models.
  • Strength: it can create, fill in missing parts, and even classify by using Bayes' rule.
  • Limit: it usually needs more data and computing power, and is harder to train.
Learns P(data)

↓Why does the critic score go down?

The critic is not getting worse; the fakes are getting better. At the start, random noise is easy to spot, so the critic is right about 98% of the time. By the end, the fakes match the real data, so the critic can only guess, like a coin flip at 50%. A falling score means the generator is winning.

~Simplified on purpose

The dots glide to their targets to show what training achieves. A real generator learns this mapping from examples, step by step.

★In the real world

Image generators, chatbots, voice cloning and realistic face synthesis are all generative models.

∞Noise is the seed

Different random noise gives a different new sample, so one trained generator can create endless variations.

Better together: the GAN

A generative adversarial network (GAN) puts both kinds of model into one game. The generator (the artist) creates gold samples, and the discriminator (the detective) is the red gate that decides what passes as real. At first, the gate catches most of the fakes. As the artist improves, almost nothing is caught.

Fakes that fool the detective: 10%
Generator (The Artist)Discriminator (The Detective)
1The game

The generator tries to fool the discriminator, while the discriminator tries to catch the generator. That rivalry is why the network is called adversarial. GANs were introduced in 2014 by Ian Goodfellow and his colleagues.

2Why it works

Every time the detective catches a fake, the artist learns what gave it away and improves. In the ideal end state, the detective can only guess, so it is right about 50% of the time.

3Where it is used

Creating realistic faces and images, sharpening blurry photos, producing extra training data, and transferring artistic styles from one picture to another.

4Watch out

GANs can be hard to train. In one common failure, called mode collapse, the generator keeps producing the same few outputs. Many newer image generators use diffusion models instead.

Discriminative models decide. Generative models create.
